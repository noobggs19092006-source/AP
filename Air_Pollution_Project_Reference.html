1. Project introduction

Problem. Given the previous 24 hours of pollutant and weather measurements from 12 Beijing monitoring stations, predict the PM2.5 pollution category at Aotizhongxin in the next hour. Each input is a tensor of shape 24 hours x 12 stations x 11 measurements = 3,168 numbers.

StateNamePM2.5 (µg/m³)
0LOWbelow 35
1MODERATE35 to below 75
2HIGH75 or more

These are project-defined hourly concentration categories, not official AQI classes or WHO thresholds.

Why this problem

  • Air pollution matters for health and the environment.
  • Measurements change with both time and place.
  • Many stations give richer history than one station alone.
  • The data form a natural multidimensional array (a tensor).
  • Tensor compression, probability and optimization together make a substantial mathematics project.
  • Question: can efficient representations support accurate future-state prediction?

Why it is difficult

  • High-dimensional inputs (3,168 numbers per sample).
  • Missing measurements.
  • Temporal dependence: this hour resembles the last.
  • Neighbouring stations are strongly correlated.
  • Pollution distributions shift over the years.
  • State changes are rare compared with "no change".
  • Nonlinear classifiers are computationally expensive.
  • Trade-offs among accuracy, reconstruction quality, runtime and storage.
  • Information leakage (the model accidentally seeing the future) must be prevented.

Objectives and contribution

We are not claiming to invent PCA, HoSVD, Markov chains, SVM or Random Fourier Features (RFF). Our scientifically defensible contribution is a careful, reproducible, leakage-controlled comparison of these established methods on one forecasting problem, including whether they detect state changes, not just overall accuracy.

Concept glossary (beginner)

Each entry: definition, why it matters, analogy, formula, and use in this project. Click to open.

Time series, feature, label

Definition: A time series is measurements recorded in time order. A feature is one input number the model sees; a label is the answer it must predict. Why: Order carries information (today depends on yesterday). Analogy: A daily diary: each page is a time step; features are what you noted, the label is what happened next. Formula: none. Here: features = 3,168 numbers per window; label = pollution state (0, 1, 2) of the next hour.

Tensor

Definition: A multi-axis array of numbers (vector = 1 axis, matrix = 2, tensor = 3 or more). Why: Keeps time, station and measurement structure instead of flattening it. Analogy: A stack of spreadsheets: each sheet is an hour, rows are stations, columns are measurements. Formula: X in R^(24 x 12 x 11); each axis is called a mode. Here: every sample is one such tensor.

Imputation

Definition: Filling in missing values with sensible substitutes. Why: Most mathematics cannot handle blanks. Analogy: A broken thermometer: you reuse the last reading for a few hours, then fall back on a typical value. Formula: none. Here: forward-fill up to 6 hours inside the same split, then station-feature medians from training data only. Missing target PM2.5 is never imputed; those samples are dropped.

Standardization

Definition: Rescaling a channel to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. Why: Otherwise large-unit channels (pressure ~1000) dominate small ones (rain). Analogy: Converting exam marks to "how many standard deviations above average". Formula: z = (x - training_mean) / training_std.

Illustrative example (made-up numbers, not project data): if a channel has training mean 80 and std 40, a reading x = 120 gives z = (120-80)/40 = 1.0, i.e. one standard deviation above typical. Validation and test reuse the training mean and std, so no future information leaks in.

Markov chain, transition matrix, stationary distribution

Definition: A system whose next-state probabilities depend only on its current state. The transition matrix P holds those probabilities; a stationary distribution π is the long-run mix of states that P leaves unchanged. Why: A simple, interpretable forecasting baseline. Analogy: Weather: "if it rained today, there is a 70% chance it rains tomorrow". Formulas: P[i,j] = C[i,j] / sum_j C[i,j] (C = observed counts i to j); πP = π, π sums to 1. Here: fitted on training transitions (Phase 3).

Eigenvalue and spectral gap

Definition: For a square matrix A, an eigenvalue λ and eigenvector v satisfy Av = λv: v is only stretched, not turned. Why: Eigenvalues of P govern how fast a Markov chain forgets its start. Analogy: Pushing a swing along its natural direction: it just swings bigger or smaller. Formula: spectral gap = 1 - |second largest eigenvalue|. Here: eigenvalues 1, 0.917412, 0.695041; gap about 0.082588 (small gap = slow mixing, very "sticky" states).

Singular value, SVD, Gram matrix

Definition: Any matrix A factors as A = U S Vᵀ; the diagonal of S holds singular values (importance of each direction). The Gram matrix is G = AᵀA, whose eigenvalues are the squared singular values. Why: It finds the most important directions in data and lets us discard the rest. Analogy: Describing a crowd by its main walking directions. Here: G is 3,168 x 3,168 (A is 24,108 x 3,168); its eigenvectors give PCA directions.

PCA

Definition: Principal Component Analysis: project centred data onto the k directions of greatest variance. Why: Compresses 3,168 numbers to k while keeping as much variation as possible. Analogy: Photographing a 3D sculpture from the angle that shows the most detail. Formula: F_PCA = flatten(Z) V_k; Z = X - M (centred), V_k = top-k eigenvectors of G. Here: k = 48, 192, 576, fitted on training only.

HoSVD and Tucker decomposition

Definition: Tucker decomposition writes a tensor as a small core tensor multiplied by one basis matrix per mode. Truncated HoSVD builds each basis from the SVD of that mode's unfolding. Why: Compresses time, station and measurement axes separately, respecting tensor structure. Analogy: PCA squashes the whole flattened pile at once; HoSVD learns separate "summaries" of the time axis, station axis and measurement axis. Formula: Core = Z x_time Q_timeᵀ x_station Q_stationᵀ x_feature Q_featureᵀ (x_mode = multiply along that axis). Here: ranks such as (8,4,6) give 8x4x6 = 192 core numbers.

Frobenius norm and reconstruction error

Definition: The Frobenius norm is the square root of the sum of squares of all entries: the "length" of a matrix or tensor. Reconstruction maps compressed features back to the original size; error measures what was lost. Analogy: Zip a photo, unzip it, measure how different it looks. Formula: RRE = ||Z - Z_hat||_F / ||Z||_F (0 = perfect; 1 = nothing recovered). Retained energy: roughly 1 - RRE² for orthogonal projections. Here: Phase 4 metric.

Optimization

Definition: Choosing parameters that minimize (or maximize) an objective. Why: Training any model is optimization. Analogy: Finding the lowest valley on a map. Here: PCA minimizes reconstruction error; SVM minimizes margin-plus-error cost (Phase 5, planned).

SVM, kernel, RBF, Random Fourier Features

SVM: a classifier that draws the separating boundary (hyperplane) with the widest safety margin. Kernel: a similarity function that lets an SVM draw curved boundaries without building the new features explicitly. RBF kernel: K(x,y) = exp(-γ||x-y||²), near 1 for close points, near 0 for distant ones (γ = how quickly similarity fades). RFF: approximates that kernel by random cosine features z(x) = sqrt(2/D) cos(Wx + b), so a fast linear SVM can mimic a nonlinear one. Analogy: kernel = judging similarity by distance; RFF = a cheap random "ruler set" approximating it. Here: planned for Phase 5 (PLANNED).

Accuracy, precision, recall, macro-F1

Accuracy: fraction of predictions correct. Precision (class c): of those predicted c, how many truly are c = TP/(TP+FP). Recall (class c): of true c cases, how many were found = TP/(TP+FN). F1: 2PR/(P+R). Macro-F1: plain average of per-class F1, so rare classes count equally. Analogy: A smoke alarm: precision = few false alarms; recall = few missed fires. Why: Accuracy alone can hide missed events (see Phase 3).

4. Phase 2: Preprocessing and tensor creation COMPLETED

Objective: turn raw hourly station data into valid, standardized, chronologically separated samples.

Step 1: Chronological splitting

  • Training: March 2013 to December 2015.
  • Validation: January to June 2016.
  • Test: July 2016 to February 2017.

Why not random? Windows overlap by 23 of 24 hours. With random splitting, a training window and a test window could share nearly identical hours, so the test would be secretly "seen" (leakage) and scores would look better than reality. Time-ordered splitting also mimics real use: train on the past, predict the future. Windows were built separately inside each split.

Step 2: Missing data and standardization

  • Forward-fill up to six hours using earlier observations in the same split.
  • Remaining missing inputs: training-only station-feature medians.
  • Standardize with training mean and std (z = (x - training_mean) / training_std); validation and test reuse these.
  • Missing target-hour PM2.5 labels: sample excluded, never imputed.

Step 3: Sliding windows

For target time t, the input is the previous 24 hours ending at t-1: X = (24, 12, 11); target y = state(observed Aotizhongxin PM2.5 at t). Consecutive windows overlap by 23 hours.

5. Phase 3: Markov chains COMPLETED

Objective: create mathematical and predictive baselines for next-hour state changes. A Markov chain says the next state's probabilities depend on the current state. Count transitions into matrix C, then normalize each row: P[i,j] = C[i,j] / sum(C[i,:]).

C = [[7144,  672,   14],     (training; 23,976 observations
     [ 618, 4465,  721],      with known previous state)
     [  57,  659, 9626]]

P = [[0.912388, 0.085824, 0.001788],
     [0.106478, 0.769297, 0.124225],
     [0.005512, 0.063721, 0.930768]]

Reading P: row = current state, column = next state. Row 0: after a LOW hour, about 91.2% stay LOW, 8.6% go MODERATE, 0.2% jump to HIGH. Diagonal entries are large (0.91, 0.77, 0.93) because pollution evolves slowly: this hour is mostly like the last. Jumps LOW to HIGH are very rare (0.0018); changes mainly step through MODERATE.

Three baselines

  1. Majority class: always predict the most frequent training label.
  2. Persistence: predict the current state continues.
  3. Modal Markov: predict the next state with the highest transition probability in the current row.

Major finding: the Markov hard predictions are identical to persistence, because the largest entry of every row is its diagonal entry.

Stationary distribution and spectrum

πP = π gives the long-run state mix: π ≈ [0.319876, 0.240439, 0.439685] (about 32% LOW, 24% MODERATE, 44% HIGH). Eigenvalues: 1, 0.917412, 0.695041. The eigenvalue 1 corresponds to π. The second eigenvalue 0.917 is close to 1, so the spectral gap (~0.082588) is small: the chain forgets its starting state slowly, matching the sticky diagonals.

Test performance (5,685 observations with known previous state)

MetricMarkov / persistence
Accuracy~90.75%
Macro-F1~0.8895
Actual state changes526

Careful interpretation. About 90.75% accuracy looks impressive, but 526 of 5,685 hours (about 9%) were changes and the model caught none of them; it can never predict a change by construction. Overall accuracy is dominated by the easy "nothing changes" hours. A smoke alarm that never rings would be "right" on all quiet days, yet useless on the day of a fire. High accuracy can therefore hide complete failure on important events.

Why probabilities beat persistence even with equal hard predictions. Persistence says "100% same state"; Markov says "91% same, 8.6% moderate, ...". The Brier score (mean squared error of the probability vector against the true one-hot outcome; lower is better) rewards honest uncertainty, hence 0.1666 < 0.1850. The probabilities also leave room for later models to use them as features.

6. Phase 4: PCA versus HoSVD COMPLETED

Objective: compare ordinary matrix dimensionality reduction (PCA) with tensor-aware reduction (truncated HoSVD, a Tucker-type decomposition). Analogy: PCA compresses a flattened collection of measurements; HoSVD separately learns compression directions along the time, station and measurement axes.

Mathematical setup

A in R^(24108 x 3168)           flattened training matrix
M = mean(X_train, axis=sample)  training-derived mean tensor
Z = X - M                       centred data
G = A^T A                       PCA Gram matrix (3168 x 3168)
F_PCA = flatten(Z) V_k          PCA features
Core = Z x_time Q_time^T x_station Q_station^T x_feature Q_feature^T

Z removes the average pattern so we compress deviations. G's top eigenvectors V_k are the best k flat directions. Q matrices are orthonormal bases for each mode (from SVDs of mode unfoldings); the Core is the compressed representation. All means and bases are fitted on training data only; validation and test are merely projected, so they never influence the compression.

Reconstruction error

RRE = ||Z - reconstructed_Z||_F / ||Z||_F. The Frobenius norm measures total size; RRE is the fraction of the data's "size" lost by compression. Lower is better.

Finding: PCA gave lower reconstruction error at all three matched feature counts.

Why: PCA searches over all k-dimensional linear subspaces of the flattened data; HoSVD is restricted to a product structure (time x station x feature bases), a smaller family. So on training data SSE_PCA(k) ≤ SSE_HoSVD(k) (SSE = sum of squared errors = ||Z - Z_hat||_F²). This is a statement about training reconstruction; it does not say PCA features will classify better, and validation/test values are not guaranteed by it.

Same total features, different reconstruction quality: the axes carry different amounts of structure. Here giving more dimensions to the measurement axis (8) helped, and giving fewer (4) hurt. Rank allocation is a real modelling choice.

Storage at 192 features

PCA model: 4,891,392 bytes. HoSVD model: 27,792 bytes (float64 mean and basis; not every application-level storage cost). PCA stores one big matrix (3,168 x 192); HoSVD stores three small matrices (24 x 8, 12 x 4, 11 x 6) plus the mean, because a product structure needs far fewer numbers than a full matrix.

Important limitations

  • About 20 redundant meteorological station-feature channels exist, including 18 bitwise duplicates. The Gram matrix (3,168 x 3,168) had numerical rank about 2,688 under its stated tolerance. Numerical near-duplicates are not necessarily exact linear dependencies; "rank" depends on the tolerance chosen.
  • A reconstruction advantage does not prove better classification.

Phase 5: SVM and kernel classification

  • Raw features + linear SVM.
  • Raw features + RBF SVM.
  • PCA features + linear / RBF SVM.
  • HoSVD features + linear / RBF SVM.
  • HoSVD features + Random Fourier Features + linear SVM.

Concepts to learn first

Soft-margin objective: min over w,b,slack: (1/2)||w||² + C Σ slack_i subject to y_i (w·x_i + b) ≥ 1 - slack_i, slack_i ≥ 0 (binary form; the 3-class problem uses a standard multiclass scheme).

  • Hyperplane: the flat boundary w·x + b = 0 separating classes.
  • Margin: the gap between the boundary and nearest points, equal to 2/||w||; minimizing ||w||² widens it.
  • Support vectors: the training points on or inside the margin; they alone determine the boundary.
  • Slack and C: slack lets points violate the margin; C sets the penalty. Large C = strict, risk of overfitting; small C = tolerant, smoother boundary.
  • Kernel trick: replace dot products by a kernel to get curved boundaries without computing the high-dimensional features.
  • Gaussian RBF kernel: K(x,y) = exp(-γ||x-y||²).
  • Random Fourier Features: approximate that kernel by randomized cosine features so a linear SVM scales better.

What we will compare

Accuracy, macro-F1, balanced accuracy, per-class precision and recall, actual state-change detection, High-state-entry detection, training time, prediction time, number of features, model memory and storage. Hyperparameters (C, γ, number of RFF features, etc.) must be chosen on validation data, never on test data. Results must be compared against the Phase 3 persistence/Markov benchmark.

Later work (plans, not results)

  • Markov information combined with SVM representations.
  • Tensor-rank sensitivity.
  • RFF approximation studies.
  • Station and feature ablations.
  • Missingness/robustness experiments.

9. Conclusions so far

  1. We identified and audited a substantial real-world environmental dataset.
  2. We built leakage-controlled, chronologically separated spatiotemporal tensors.
  3. We established and mathematically investigated a Markov-chain baseline.
  4. We found that high baseline accuracy can hide complete failure to anticipate state changes (0 of 526 changes detected).
  5. We experimentally compared PCA and HoSVD dimensionality reduction.
  6. PCA reconstructed the standardized input data more accurately.
  7. HoSVD had substantially smaller projection-model storage.
  8. Rank allocation changed HoSVD reconstruction quality.
  9. We have not yet shown which representation is best for classification.
  10. Phase 5 will address that question.

How I Would Explain My Project to My Professor

(about 2-3 minutes spoken)

"My project asks whether we can predict the next hour's PM2.5 pollution category in Beijing from the last 24 hours of data at 12 monitoring stations. Each input is a tensor of 24 hours by 12 stations by 11 measurements, 3,168 numbers, and the target is a project-defined Low, Moderate or High state at Aotizhongxin. I use the UCI Beijing Multi-Site dataset, 35,064 hours per station.

First I audited the data: no duplicates, a few percent missing. In preprocessing I split chronologically into training, validation and test periods, because overlapping windows would leak information under random splitting. Missing values were forward-filled for up to six hours and then replaced by training-only medians, and everything was standardized with training statistics. That gave 34,067 samples.

Then I built a Markov-chain baseline. Its transition matrix has large diagonal entries, so its hard predictions are identical to persistence, with about 90.75% test accuracy and macro-F1 near 0.89. But it detected none of the 526 actual state changes and none of 154 entries into High. So accuracy alone is misleading, and any new model must be judged on transitions as well.

In Phase 4 I compared PCA, which compresses the flattened data, with truncated HoSVD, which compresses time, station and measurement axes separately. PCA reconstructed better at every size, for example validation error 0.288 versus 0.388 at 192 features, which agrees with theory. But the HoSVD model needs only 27,792 bytes versus 4,891,392 for PCA, and the way ranks are distributed across the axes matters.

I have not yet shown which representation classifies best. Phase 5 will train linear, RBF and Random Fourier Feature SVMs on raw, PCA and HoSVD features, choose hyperparameters on validation only, and compare accuracy, macro-F1, change detection, runtime and storage. My contribution is a careful, reproducible comparison of established methods, not a new method."
